\begin{abstract}
When one rotor of a quadrotor (a drone with four propellers) stops completely, the drone can no longer hover level. The only steady way it can still fly is to spin continuously about a slightly tilted axis. Mueller and D'Andrea called this state the \emph{relaxed hover} and showed that it can be controlled. This project studies, in simulation, how to bring a drone into this spinning state reliably after a rotor fails.

We ask two connected questions. \textbf{First}, the physics model of the spinning drone is good but not perfect: can we keep it and learn only its error from data, with the tools of Koopman methods, and does this predict and control better than the physics model alone or a Koopman model of the full dynamics? \textbf{Second}, a fault detector needs time to notice the failure: how long may this take before a safe recovery is no longer possible, and can such a limit be guaranteed mathematically?

Our simulator of the Mueller--D'Andrea drone reproduces their two-rotor and three-rotor-loss equilibria to every published digit, five hover solutions of their more detailed model within 4\%, and the spin-up time of their flight within about 4\%.

\textbf{Learning the model error.} For prediction horizons from 100\,ms to 1\,s, the model that learns only the error (the \emph{residual} model) is the most accurate predictor on the plant it was trained on, and with 10 training episodes it beats a full Koopman model trained on 40. Inside model predictive control (MPC) it reduces a steady altitude error caused by model mismatch by about one third. An offset-free MPC that estimates a disturbance on all physical states removes the steady error but fails the capture even without detection delay, while an LQR with an integrator reduces it to at most 0.024\,m and keeps the capture performance of the LQR (safe up to 10\,ms): the residual is not the best way to remove the error, and the integrator LQR is the best trade-off we tested. An MPC on the full-state Koopman model is stable near the hover but fails the capture earlier. The learned models do not work on a plant with different parameters; online adaptation halves the prediction error there and repairs the worst closed-loop case but is no better on the training plant, a residual trained on capture transients predicts them an order of magnitude better but is a worse capture controller, longer MPC horizons do not help, and a residual trained on noisy measurements is harmful. These conclusions hold on truth plants that also contain air-relative thrust and quadratic drag.

\textbf{Detection delay.} In our simulation, the published recovery strategy recovers safely with probability above 0.9 only if the failure is detected within about 9 to 12\,ms, while published detectors need about 20 to 130\,ms. A first certificate based on linear matrix inequalities covers 22\% of the way from the spinning equilibrium to the state right after a failure. We then extended it to a quasi-linear parameter-varying model that is valid over a range of spin rates and verified it numerically and by 2000 nonlinear simulations, and examined the state at which the published strategy hands over from its open-loop spin-up to the failure controller. That state is far from the spinning equilibrium (the spin rate is 9\,rad/s too low and the attitude is tilted by 0.75 even without delay), none of 1312 simulated hand-over states lies inside the first certificate, and a geometric argument shows that no ellipsoid centred at the equilibrium can contain it. Once the nonlinear remainder is bounded the certified sets are small, a synthesised capture gain gives larger certified sets but only with closed-loop poles faster than the motors can follow, and the centre of the hand-over set is outside every certificate we computed: a certified detection-delay budget does not exist in any configuration we tested. The residual Koopman MPC loop cannot be certified either. We report these as negative results.

Technical terms are explained where they first appear and are collected in a glossary (Appendix~\ref{app:glossary}). Each method is described in three layers: the idea in plain words, the mathematics, and how it is implemented in our code.
\end{abstract}
