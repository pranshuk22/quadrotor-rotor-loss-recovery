\begin{abstract}
When one rotor of a quadrotor (a drone with four propellers) stops completely, the drone can no longer hover level. The only steady way it can still fly is to spin continuously about a slightly tilted axis. Mueller and D'Andrea called this state the \emph{relaxed hover} and showed that it can be controlled. This project studies, in simulation, how to control a drone in this spinning state when the model it is controlled with is not exact.

The physics model of the spinning drone is good but not perfect. We ask: can we keep it and learn only its error from data, with the tools of Koopman methods, and does this predict and control the failed drone better than the physics model alone or a Koopman model of the full dynamics?

Our simulator of the Mueller--D'Andrea drone reproduces their two-rotor and three-rotor-loss equilibria to every published digit, five hover solutions of their more detailed model within 4\%, and the spin-up time of their flight within about 4\%. For prediction horizons from 100\,ms to 1\,s, the model that learns only the error (the \emph{residual} model) is the most accurate predictor on the plant it was trained on, and with 10 training episodes it beats a full Koopman model trained on 40. Inside model predictive control (MPC) it reduces a steady altitude error caused by model mismatch by about one third.

Simple classical baselines do better. An LQR with an integrator reduces the same error to at most 0.024\,m and still catches the drone after a failure as well as the plain LQR does (safely for detection delays up to 10\,ms). An offset-free MPC removes the error completely, but it loses the drone during the recovery from the failure, even when the failure is detected at once. An MPC built on the full-state Koopman model is stable near the hover, but in the recovery it is no safer than the other MPCs and on the training plant it diverges completely from a detection delay of 20\,ms on.

The learned models also do not carry over to a drone with different parameters. Updating the residual online halves the prediction error on such a drone, but does not help on the drone it was trained on. Training on recovery transients improves prediction but makes the controller worse, longer MPC horizons do not help, and training on noisy measurements is harmful. All of these conclusions also hold when the simulated drone includes air-relative thrust and quadratic drag.

Technical terms are explained where they first appear and are collected in a glossary (Appendix~\ref{app:glossary}). Each method is described in three layers: the idea in plain words, the mathematics, and how it is implemented in our code.
\end{abstract}
