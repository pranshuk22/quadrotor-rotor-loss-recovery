\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
%=====================================================================

\subsection{How a quadrotor flies}
A quadrotor has four propellers, each driven by its own electric motor. Each propeller pushes air down and so produces an upward force called \emph{thrust}. A quadrotor has no wings and no rudder: everything it does, it does by changing the four thrusts.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Going up and down.} If all four thrusts together are larger than the weight, the drone climbs; if smaller, it descends.
  \item \textbf{Tilting (roll and pitch).} If the propeller on one side pushes harder than the one on the opposite side, the drone tilts. A tilted drone has its thrust pointing partly sideways, so it moves sideways. This is how a quadrotor travels horizontally.
  \item \textbf{Turning (yaw).} A spinning propeller also pushes back on the drone with a small twisting effect (a \emph{reaction torque}), in the direction opposite to its spin. Two of the propellers spin clockwise and two anticlockwise, so in normal flight the twisting effects cancel. To turn, the drone speeds up the pair that spins one way and slows down the other pair.
\end{itemize}
Four thrusts control exactly four things: total upward force, roll, pitch and yaw. There is no spare actuator. This is why losing even one rotor is serious.

\subsection{What happens when a rotor fails}
If one rotor stops completely, only three thrusts remain, but the drone still needs to (i) carry its weight, (ii) keep from rolling over and (iii) cancel the yaw twist. With three propellers this is impossible: the reaction torques of the remaining propellers no longer cancel, so the drone starts to spin about its vertical axis, faster and faster, until air drag on the spinning body balances the twist.

Mueller and D'Andrea showed that this is not the end~\cite{mueller2014stability,mueller2016relaxed}. If we \emph{give up} control of the yaw angle (the compass heading) and accept that the drone spins, the remaining three rotors can still hold the drone in the air and steer it. For their drone the model predicts a constant spin of about 20\,rad/s (about 3 turns per second) around an axis tilted about 17$^\circ$ from the body's vertical axis, while the centre of mass moves on a tiny circle of about 8\,mm radius (our simulator gives 19.6\,rad/s, 16$^\circ$ and 7\,mm; Section~\ref{sec:validation} explains the small difference). They called this state the \emph{relaxed hover}, and they flew it on a real drone.

\begin{plain}
A spinning top cannot stand upright at rest but is stable while it spins. After a rotor loss the drone behaves in a similar way: it is stable only while it spins, and it moves by tilting its spin axis.
\end{plain}

\subsection{The problem this project studies}
\paragraph{The model is not perfect.} A controller is designed using a mathematical model of the drone. The physics model of Mueller and D'Andrea captures the main spinning motion well, but it misses some real effects. In their two-propeller flight the drone stayed about 2.4\,m below its set-point, which the authors attribute to less thrust while spinning fast, and in the one-propeller flights the drone spun about 20 to 27\% slower than predicted~\cite{mueller2014stability,mueller2016relaxed}. The inertia and the motor response are never known exactly either. A controller built on an imperfect model makes systematic errors; for example, it settles at the wrong height.

A natural fix is to keep the physics model and \emph{learn its error} from data. We learn the error with the tools of Koopman methods (EDMD, Section~\ref{sec:background}): a fixed dictionary of nonlinear features of the state and input, and least-squares regression. Following Todde et al.~\cite{todde2026residual}, the controller evaluates the learned correction outside its optimization, so the optimization problem stays convex and easy to solve. Such ``residual Koopman'' models already exist for healthy drones and other robots~\cite{todde2026residual,bruder2025residual,narayanan2026rkmpc}, but we did not find them applied to the spinning state after a complete rotor loss.


\subsection{Research question}
\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item[Research question.] Can a Koopman-style (EDMD) model of only the mismatch, built on top of the known spinning-equilibrium model of Mueller and D'Andrea, predict and control the failed drone better than the physics model alone, or than a Koopman model of the full dynamics? ``Better'' means a smaller multi-step prediction error on new data, and a smaller steady error in closed loop.
\end{description}
A controller for the failed drone is only useful if it can also take over right after the failure, so every controller is also tested on how well it catches the drone after a detection delay (Section~\ref{sec:delay}).

\paragraph{Hypotheses.} Before the experiments we wrote down what we expected:
\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=1pt]
  \item[H1.] A Koopman model of the full failed-drone dynamics has eigenvalues outside the unit circle and loses to a ``nothing changes'' predictor at long horizons.
  \item[H2.] The residual model has a smaller multi-step prediction error than both the physics model and the full Koopman model.
  \item[H3.] An MPC that uses the residual removes the steady error that model mismatch causes in an MPC built on the physics model.
  \item[H4.] The residual model reaches a given prediction error with less training data than the full Koopman model.
\end{description}
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} gives the status of each.

\subsection{What we did}
Everything is done in simulation; no hardware is used. In order:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item We wrote a simulator of the drone used by Mueller and D'Andrea (Sections~\ref{sec:model} and~\ref{sec:code}).
  \item \textbf{Validation:} we checked it against three published papers, against flight data and against hand-derived physics results (Section~\ref{sec:validation}).
  \item \textbf{Linear analysis:} we analysed the linearized models (controllability, observability) and designed LQR controllers (Section~\ref{sec:linear}).
  \item \textbf{Prediction:} we built three predictors of the failed drone (physics only, full Koopman, residual Koopman) and compared them (Section~\ref{sec:koopman}).
  \item \textbf{MPC:} we built MPC controllers on the physics model with and without the learned residual (Section~\ref{sec:mpc}).
  \item \textbf{Classical baselines:} we compared the residual Koopman MPC with two classical ways of removing a steady error, an offset-free MPC and an LQR with an integrator (Section~\ref{sec:offsetfree}).
  \item \textbf{Full-state Koopman MPC:} we built an MPC on the full-state Koopman model and compared all three MPCs (Section~\ref{sec:fullkoopmanmpc}).
  \item \textbf{Further tests:} we tested whether online adaptation of the residual, transient training data, other MPC horizons and weights, other modelling choices and a more realistic truth plant change the conclusions (Section~\ref{sec:depth}).
\end{enumerate}
Appendix~\ref{app:repro} lists the script that produces each result.

\paragraph{What is reproduction and what is new.} The model, the equilibrium, its controllability, the LQR weights and the spin-up strategy are from Mueller and D'Andrea; we reimplemented and checked them, and found one inconsistency in a published case. New in this work, as far as our literature search found (Section~\ref{sec:literature}): a controlled comparison of physics-only, full Koopman and residual models, and of the MPCs built on them, in the spinning regime after complete rotor loss, together with classical offset-free and integral baselines, online adaptation, training on transients, ablations and a more realistic simulated drone. Not new: the residual Koopman idea itself~\cite{todde2026residual} and offset-free control of Koopman models for other systems~\cite{schimperna2025offsetfree}.
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses the results, the limitations and future work. Appendix~\ref{app:glossary} is a glossary of all technical terms, Appendix~\ref{app:symbols} lists the symbols, and Appendix~\ref{app:repro} explains how to reproduce every number.
