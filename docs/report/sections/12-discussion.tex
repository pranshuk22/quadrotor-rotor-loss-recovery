\subsection{What the results mean}
\paragraph{Physics first, learning second.} The physics model of Mueller and D'Andrea, which we reproduced to every printed digit except in one internally inconsistent published case, already describes the spinning drone well: it gives the right equilibrium, the right flight spin-up time and a controller that works. Learning helps only in the second order, and only when the learned part is restricted to the error. Learning everything from data produced an unstable model, as our proposal predicted.

\paragraph{A calibration, not yet a general model.} The learned residual is accurate on the plant it saw and harmful on a different plant. As it stands it behaves like an offline calibration of one vehicle. Online adaptation, or a residual that also uses a short history of states, is needed before it can be called a general correction for rotor failure.

\paragraph{Integral action is the best bias remedy tested.} Among all controllers of E7 and E8, the LQR with an integrator is the best trade-off: it removes the steady height error (at most 0.024\,m) and leaves the capture performance of the LQR unchanged, and it beats the residual Koopman MPC on both counts. The residual is therefore not the best way to remove the bias; its remaining value, if any, lies in transients or in effects that integral action cannot see, which we did not test.

\paragraph{Detection time is the bottleneck.} The most striking result is how little detection delay the published strategy tolerates in our simulation: around 10 to 20\,ms, while published detectors need about 20 to 130\,ms. The cause is simple and robust: the healthy controller cannot stop the roll because the opposite rotor cannot go below idle thrust. This makes a certified delay budget, and controllers that tolerate longer delays, practically important.

\paragraph{The hand-over, not the capture controller, is where the guarantee fails.} The published strategy flies the first 0.65\,s open loop and only then hands over to the failure LQR. At that moment the drone is far from the relaxed hover (spin rate 9\,rad/s too low, attitude tilted by 0.75), the LQR recovers from there in simulation, and no certificate we could build contains the state: the first LTI certificate covers 0.23 of the way, the quasi-LPV certificate that is valid over a range of spin rates covers at most 0.0032 once its nonlinear remainder is bounded, and a symmetry argument shows that no ellipsoid centred at the equilibrium can contain it. Designing a better gain with the certificate framework did not help, because the best certified extent (0.46) comes with a gain that the motors cannot follow. We therefore conclude that a certified delay budget for the published strategy is not within reach of ellipsoidal certificates of this kind; the empirical budget, changing the hand-over threshold, or a different certificate family (for example a non-symmetric or polyhedral set, or sum-of-squares sets) are the open routes.

\paragraph{Learning the model error: what survived.} The residual model is accurate and data-efficient on the plant it was trained on, and it removes part of a steady error inside a convex MPC. Everything we added to make it more general or more capable did not change that picture in a useful way: online adaptation repairs the worst held-out case but not the prediction on the training plant, training on transients makes the capture worse, a longer horizon or a heavier attitude weight changes capture by at most one delay, and noisy training data are harmful. For the practical goal (a small steady error and a safe capture) a classical integrator is better than any learned variant tested.

\subsection{Limitations}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Spin-up thrust.} The delay tolerance depends on the spin-up thrust (12 against 7\,ms for the 90\% level). The flight value is read from a plot and carries some uncertainty.
  \item \textbf{Nominal plant for the delay study.} The delay study uses the nominal plant; the published models over-predict the steady spin rate in flight by about 20 to 27\%.
  \item \textbf{Generalization.} The residual model helps reliably only on the plant family it was trained on.
  \item \textbf{Certificates.} The first certificate holds for the linearized loop. The quasi-LPV certificates are for the nominal plant; their nonlinear remainder is bounded by sampling and local optimization, not proven; the remainder-free tier is certified for the model only; all are checked by nonlinear simulation, which can falsify but not prove.
  \item \textbf{Truth-plant realism.} Families A and B add thrust loss, inertia error and motor lag; families A$^+$ and B$^+$ also add air-relative thrust and quadratic drag (Section~\ref{sec:realistic}). There is no translational drag, and sensor noise was studied only for the residual and the MPC (Section~\ref{sec:ablations}), not for the certificates.
  \item \textbf{Certificate for the learned controller.} The clipping level $\vect d_{\max}$ is a percentile of the training residuals and the plant mismatch is bounded empirically; the certificate of Section~\ref{sec:mpccert} fails even so, and the volume of its ellipsoid was not confirmed by a second solver.
  \item \textbf{Simulation only.} No result has been tested on hardware.
  \item \textbf{Full-state measurement.} All controllers are given the exact state. A real spinning drone needs an estimator for the tilt, height and vertical speed, and sensor noise will affect both the learned residual and the MPC.
  \item \textbf{Closed-loop data.} The learning data are recorded under LQR feedback with added excitation, which correlates states and inputs; possible bias from this is not analysed.
  \item \textbf{Missing baselines.} Grey-box estimation of the few uncertain physical parameters (thrust loss, inertias, motor time constant) is the obvious model-based alternative to a black-box residual; it has not been compared. The offset-free baselines of Section~\ref{sec:offsetfree} were compared on one start per plant.
  \item \textbf{Literature access.} Several closely related papers~\cite{stephan2018lpv,lanzon2014flight,qi2016recoverable,basak2015switching,rotondo2016virtual} were not available to us in an open version and have not been read in full; our statements about them rely on abstracts, a thesis and citing papers, and our novelty statements are the outcome of a search.
  \item \textbf{Platform and runs.} Some of the long experiments were interrupted or re-run (Section~\ref{sec:budget-dev}); the experiments of Section~\ref{sec:pending} were not finished when this version was written.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Future work}
The following items are not part of the results above.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A certificate family that can contain the hand-over state:} non-symmetric or polyhedral invariant sets, sum-of-squares sets for the nonlinear loop, or a parameter-dependent Lyapunov matrix; with a rigorous (not sampled) bound on the nonlinear remainder.
  \item \textbf{Hand-over design:} the effect of the hand-over threshold on the delay budget (Section~\ref{sec:redesign}) deserves a proper analysis and a confirmation on a plant with realistic mismatch.
  \item \textbf{Offset-free action that survives the capture:} the state-model offset-free MPC fails the capture after the hand-over (Section~\ref{sec:offsetfree}); switching the estimator on only after the capture is an untested suggestion.
  \item \textbf{Grey-box estimation} of the few uncertain physical parameters (thrust loss, inertias, motor time constant) as the model-based alternative to a black-box residual, and a correction for the bias of fits on noisy data (for example total least squares).
  \item \textbf{Output feedback:} an estimator for the tilt, height and vertical speed from realistic sensors, and its effect on the learned residual and on the certificates.
  \item \textbf{Hardware:} nothing here has been tested on a real drone.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Conclusion}
We built a simulator of a quadrotor with complete rotor loss and showed that it reproduces three published papers and the spin-up time of a real flight. On the plant it was trained on, a Koopman model that learns only the error of the physics model is the most accurate predictor from 100\,ms to 1\,s, needs less data than a Koopman model of the full dynamics, and reduces a steady altitude error by one third inside a convex MPC. It does not carry over to a plant with different parameters (online adaptation helps only partly), and for removing a steady error an LQR with an integrator is better than every learned variant we tested, while the MPC on the full-state Koopman model fails the capture earlier. These findings hold on truth plants with air-relative thrust and quadratic drag.

In our simulation, the published recovery strategy recovers safely with high probability only for detection delays of about 10 to 20\,ms, shorter than most published detectors need (about 20 to 130\,ms), because nothing can stop the roll while the failure is undetected. We built a saturation-aware certificate over a range of spin rates, verified it numerically and by nonlinear simulation, and used it to ask whether the state at which the strategy hands over to the failure controller can be certified. It cannot: that state is far from the equilibrium, no ellipsoid centred at the equilibrium can contain it, the certified sets are small once the nonlinear remainder is bounded, a synthesised gain gives larger sets only with poles the motors cannot follow, and the residual Koopman loop cannot be certified either. A certified detection-delay budget does not exist for any configuration we tested. We report this as the main negative result, together with the finding that changing the hand-over threshold changes the empirical delay budget by an order of magnitude (to be confirmed, Section~\ref{sec:pending}), which points to the hand-over design as the most promising direction.
