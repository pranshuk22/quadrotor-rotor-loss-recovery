\section{Model predictive control with the learned residual}\label{sec:mpc}
%=====================================================================
This section answers the control half of our research question. It first tests hypothesis \textbf{H3}: a residual Koopman MPC removes the steady error that model mismatch causes in a controller designed on the physics model. It then compares the residual Koopman MPC with two classical ways of removing a steady error (Section~\ref{sec:offsetfree}) and with an MPC built on the full-state Koopman model (Section~\ref{sec:fullkoopmanmpc}).

\subsection{The MPC problem}
At every sample (5\,ms) the controller measures the current state, builds $\vect s_0 = (\vect x, \hat{\vect f})$ and solves
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mpc}
\begin{aligned}
\min_{\vect u_0,\dots,\vect u_{N-1}} \;& \textstyle\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\big(\vect s_j^\top Q\,\vect s_j + \vect u_j^\top R\,\vect u_j\big) + \vect s_N^\top P_N\,\vect s_N\\
\text{subject to }\;& \vect s_{j+1} = A_d\vect s_j + B_d\vect u_j + \hat{\vect d},\qquad j = 0,\dots,N-1,\\
& f_{\min}-\bar f_i \;\le\; u_{j,i} \;\le\; f_{\max}-\bar f_i .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The prediction model is the 10-state analytical model of Section~\ref{sec:koopman}.
  \item The horizon is $N = 20$ steps of 5\,ms, so the controller looks 100\,ms ahead.
  \item $Q$ and $R$ are the same weights as the failure LQR, so the two controllers aim for the same thing.
  \item The \emph{terminal cost} $P_N$ is the solution of the discrete-time Riccati equation for $(A_d, B_d, Q, R)$. It stands in for the cost after the horizon; with it, the MPC behaves like the discrete LQR whenever the limits are not active, which our tests check.
  \item The input limits are the motor thrust limits, written as deviations from the trim.
\end{itemize}
Only the first input $\vect u_0$ is applied; at the next sample the problem is solved again from the new measurement.

\subsection{Where the learned residual enters}
The \textbf{analytical MPC} uses $\hat{\vect d} = 0$. The \textbf{residual Koopman MPC} evaluates the learned residual once per sample, at the current state and previous input, \emph{outside} the optimization, and adds it to every predicted step as a known constant $\hat{\vect d}$ (a ``measured disturbance''), as Todde et al. do~\cite{todde2026residual}. The learned part is therefore not a decision variable, the constraints stay linear, and~\eqref{eq:mpc} stays a convex QP that solves reliably every time. The price is that the optimizer does not know how the residual would change with its own choice of inputs or along the predicted trajectory: the correction is frozen at its current value for the whole 100\,ms horizon.

\begin{plain}
The physics model plans the next 100\,ms; the learned residual tells it ``at the moment the real drone drifts this much more per step than you think'', and the plan compensates for that drift.
\end{plain}

\begin{codebox}{building and solving the QP once per sample}
\begin{lstlisting}
# built once: CVXPY parameters x0 and d, variables X (states) and U (inputs)
for j in range(N):
    cons += [X[:, j + 1] == Ad @ X[:, j] + Bd @ U[:, j] + self.d,
             U[:, j] >= lo, U[:, j] <= hi]
    cost += cp.quad_form(X[:, j], Q) + cp.quad_form(U[:, j], R)
cost += cp.quad_form(X[:, N], PN)                       # DARE terminal cost

# every sample
d[:7] = self.residual.residual(x7, self.fe, self.u_prev) # learned part, outside the QP
self.x0.value, self.d.value = np.concatenate((x7, self.fe)), d
self.prob.solve(solver=cp.OSQP, warm_start=True, eps_abs=1e-5, eps_rel=1e-5)
u = self.U.value[:, 0]                                   # apply the first move only
\end{lstlisting}
\end{codebox}
The problem is modelled with CVXPY~\cite{diamond2016cvxpy} and solved with OSQP~\cite{stellato2020osqp}, an operator-splitting QP solver; ``warm start'' reuses the previous solution as the starting guess.

\subsection{Experiment: steady altitude error under mismatch}
All three controllers (LQR, analytical MPC, residual Koopman MPC) are designed on the nominal parameters and flown on the mismatched truth plants. The residual is the one trained on family A in Section~\ref{sec:koopman}. Each run starts 1\,m below the set-point near the relaxed hover and lasts 8\,s; the steady error is the mean height over the last 2\,s. We use one run per plant and controller, because the steady state does not depend on the starting point. To see which effect causes the error, we also test each family without its thrust loss, and the nominal plant with only that family's thrust loss.

\paragraph{Results.} On plant A, LQR and the analytical MPC both settle 0.29\,m too low; they are practically identical, because near the equilibrium the thrust limits are not reached and the MPC then acts like the LQR. The thrust loss alone causes 0.10\,m and the inertia and motor mismatch alone 0.15\,m; the effects are not exactly additive (together 0.29\,m). The residual Koopman MPC settles 0.19\,m low, a 32\% smaller error, and it reduces the error for each separate effect of plant A. On the held-out plant B the effects partly cancel. The residual MPC still reduces the error for the full plant and for its thrust loss alone, but when B's thrust loss is removed it makes the error 47\% \emph{larger}: the correction learned on A pushes in the wrong direction for that plant. This is the generalization problem of Section~\ref{sec:koopman} again. H3 is therefore only partly supported: on the training plant the error is reduced by 32\%, not removed, and on a held-out variant it grows. The errors on plant B are only a few centimetres, so their percentages should not be over-read.

\paragraph{Capture after a detection delay.} In the capture test of Section~\ref{sec:delay} on family A, the analytical and the residual MPC are safe only when the failure is detected at once (height loss 0.53 and 0.58\,m), whereas the LQR is also safe at 10\,ms (1.69\,m). From 20\,ms on all three fail, and the MPCs lose more height than the LQR (at 30\,ms: 19.2 and 19.5\,m against 12.6\,m). The residual therefore does not help the capture. Section~\ref{sec:tuning} checks whether this depends on the MPC settings.

The residual reduces the steady error but does not remove it. The classical way to remove a steady error caused by a constant mismatch is integral action, or an ``offset-free'' MPC that estimates a constant disturbance from the measurements. Section~\ref{sec:offsetfree} compares against these baselines.

\paragraph{Computation time.} The QP solver needs a median of 0.73\,ms per sample. Through the CVXPY modelling layer the median wall-clock time is 4.4\,ms, which leaves almost no margin to the 5\,ms sample period. A real-time version would call the solver directly.

\subsection{Classical baselines: offset-free MPC and integral action}\label{sec:offsetfree}
\begin{plain}
An offset-free MPC adds to the model a constant, unknown push $\vect\delta$, estimates it from the measurements with a filter, and plans around it: it asks for the steady state in which the estimated push is cancelled and the height and tilt are exactly at their set-points. Integral action does the same job more cheaply: the LQR adds the accumulated height error as an extra state.
\end{plain}
\paragraph{Design.} All variants use the same weights, horizon, terminal cost and thrust limits as the analytical MPC, and all settings were fixed before the runs. The prediction model is the nominal 10-state model with a constant disturbance added, $\vect s^+ = A_d\vect s + B_d\vect u + G\vect\delta + \vect d_{\mathrm{res}}$ and $\vect\delta^+ = \vect\delta$, where $\vect d_{\mathrm{res}}$ is the learned residual when it is used and zero otherwise. A steady-state Kalman filter (a filter that weighs the model against the measurement with fixed noise levels) estimates $\vect\delta$. From the estimate, the controller computes a \emph{target}: the steady state $(\vect s_s,\vect u_s)$ of the model in which $n_x$, $n_y$ and the height sit at their set-points (if the thrust limits forbid it, a small QP picks the closest feasible one). The MPC then steers the filtered state estimate to this target. We tried two disturbance models:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Thrust model}: $\vect\delta$ has three entries, an offset on the thrust produced by rotors 1 to 3, and $G$ is the discretized thrust input matrix of the 7-state model. The augmented model is detectable (rank 13, smallest singular value 0.027), so the offsets can be estimated.
  \item \textbf{State model}: $\vect\delta$ has seven entries, one constant offset per physical state, and $G = [I_7;0]$.
\end{itemize}
Both are also combined with the residual: the residual enters the prediction, and the filter removes what is left. The classical reference is the failure LQR with an integrator on the height error (integrator weight 1; the integrator is frozen while a command is at its limit).

\paragraph{Why two disturbance models.} The thrust model was our first design. On plant A it left a height error of $-0.24$\,m, hardly better than the LQR. The reason is structural. With seven measured physical states but only three disturbance states, the filter can come to rest while part of the prediction error is still unexplained, and the loop then settles with an error. With one disturbance state per physical state this cannot happen: at rest the prediction error of every physical state is zero, and so is the height error. We added the state model after this first result and report both; no setting was tuned.

\paragraph{Results near the hover.} The state-model offset-free MPC removes the steady height error on all six plants (0.000\,m to three decimals). It also removes the tilt error (at most $10^{-4}$, against 0.005 to 0.035 for the LQR) and settles to within $\pm5$\,cm in about 1.8\,s, whereas the LQR does not settle within 8\,s on five of the six plants. The LQR with an integrator brings the error down to at most 0.024\,m; it settles more slowly (1.5 to 5.5\,s) and leaves the tilt error of the LQR unchanged, because only the height is integrated. The thrust model does not remove the error on the full plants ($-0.239$\,m on A, $+0.165$\,m on B, worse than the LQR on B). Combined with the residual it removes the error on plant A and on A without thrust loss, but leaves 0.19 to 0.35\,m on the other four plants.

Against the working baselines, the residual Koopman MPC is clearly not the best way to remove a steady error: it leaves $-0.193$\,m on plant A, where the state-model offset-free MPC leaves 0.000\,m. H3 is therefore supported only in a weak sense: the residual reduces the error, but integral action and offset-free MPC remove it without any learning. Adding the residual to the state-model offset-free MPC changes nothing visible, because the filter removes whatever the residual leaves.

\paragraph{Capture after a detection delay.} Table~\ref{tab:e8a2} repeats the capture test with these controllers on family A. The integrator LQR behaves like the LQR and is safe up to 10\,ms. The thrust-model offset-free MPC and its residual version behave like the other MPCs and are safe only at 0\,ms. The two \textbf{state-model controllers fail at every delay, even at 0\,ms}: the drone turns over and loses 14.3\,m and 10.7\,m of height. They also fail from 0\,ms on the nominal plant. (This experiment had no rule that stops a diverging run, so height losses above 100\,m are shown as ``$>$100''.)

\paragraph{Why the offset-free MPC fails the capture.} To see what goes wrong, we logged one capture of the state-model controller on the nominal plant with no detection delay. The controller takes over 0.65\,s after the failure, while the yaw rate is still 8.8\,rad/s below its steady value, and the drone tips past horizontal ($n_z<0$) only 0.215\,s later. During this time the target always stays within the thrust limits, but the filter reads a large part of the fast motion of the drone as a constant disturbance: the estimated offsets of the tilt $n_y$ and of the vertical speed reach 73\% and 59\% of the largest one-step change of those states. The target thrusts move away from the trim by up to 1.7\,N within 0.22\,s, and the commands run into their limits. We believe the cause is that the linear model is poor this far from the relaxed hover, so that the filter mistakes model error for a disturbance, but we have not tested this. The estimator that makes the controller offset-free near the hover is therefore harmful during the capture. Switching it on only after the capture is a natural next step that we did not test.

\paragraph{The best trade-off.} Among all controllers tested here, the \textbf{LQR with an integrator is the best trade-off}. It brings the steady height error down to at most 0.024\,m on all six plants and keeps the capture performance of the LQR (safe up to 10\,ms). It beats the residual Koopman MPC on both counts: the residual MPC leaves up to 0.19\,m on plant A and is safe only at 0\,ms.

\paragraph{Computation time.} The offset-free MPC needs a median of 4.2 to 4.6\,ms per step through CVXPY (0.7 to 0.9\,ms inside OSQP), about the same as the analytical MPC. All QPs were solved to optimality.

\subsection{MPC on the full-state Koopman model}\label{sec:fullkoopmanmpc}
\begin{plain}
The third MPC does not use the physics model at all: it plans with the lifted linear model learned from data in Section~\ref{sec:koopman}. At every sample it lifts the measured state, predicts the next 100\,ms with the lifted model, and chooses the thrusts that minimize the same cost as the other MPCs.
\end{plain}
\paragraph{Design.} The prediction model is the lifted model of Section~\ref{sec:koopman} (55 lifted states) plus a height state that integrates the vertical speed. The lifted state is recomputed from the measurement at every sample rather than carried over from the previous one, as is usual in Koopman MPC; this keeps model errors from accumulating. The cost uses the same weights as the other MPCs, plus a tiny weight of $10^{-6}$ on the lifted state so that the QP is strictly convex. The terminal cost is the solution of the discrete Riccati equation of the lifted model. The constant coordinate of the dictionary (an eigenvalue of exactly 1, always zero in deviation coordinates) makes that equation very ill-conditioned (condition number $8\times10^{14}$), so it is left out of the terminal cost. The solution then passes the usual checks (relative residual $2\times10^{-15}$, symmetric, positive semi-definite, closed-loop spectral radius 0.9949). As in Section~\ref{sec:koopman}, four of the 55 eigenvalues of the lifted model lie outside the unit circle (largest modulus 1.0070).

\paragraph{Results near the hover.} Although the lifted model has unstable modes, re-lifting at every sample keeps the loop stable. The controller holds the height within 0.19\,m of the set-point on plant A, and its steady errors are close to those of the residual Koopman MPC on every plant (Table~\ref{tab:e8b}), including the same growth on plant B without thrust loss ($+0.178$\,m against $+0.112$\,m for the LQR). Both learned controllers therefore act as calibrations to plant A, and the better prediction accuracy of the residual model (Section~\ref{sec:koopman}) brings no visible advantage here. The lifted model also carries a bias: at the exact trim the controller commands $(+0.066, -0.450, +0.124)$\,N away from the relaxed-hover thrusts, where the correct command is zero.

\paragraph{Capture after a detection delay.} With no delay the full-state Koopman MPC captures the drone safely on both plants (0.74\,m of height loss on A, minimum $n_z$ 0.08; 0.03\,m on the nominal plant). At 10\,ms it is unsafe on plant A (4.96\,m, $n_z$ down to $-0.26$) and safe on the nominal plant (0.90\,m), like the analytical MPC. Beyond that it fails much more badly than the other MPCs: from 20\,ms on plant A and from 30\,ms on the nominal plant its runs diverge (height error above 100\,m) within 3.95 to 7.19\,s, whereas the analytical and residual MPC runs diverge only at 60\,ms, if at all. During these captures the state leaves the range of the training data: the largest standardized feature reaches 34 to 350 standard deviations, against 8 to 9 near the hover, so the quadratic features are extrapolated far beyond anything the model has seen. This matches our expectation that a model learned entirely from data near the hover cannot be trusted during the large transients of the capture; there the physics model is needed.

\paragraph{Computation time.} The QP with 56 states and a 20-step horizon takes a median of 55 to 89\,ms per step near the hover and up to 319\,ms during capture through CVXPY, far above the 5\,ms period. As implemented, this controller is therefore a tool for analysis, not a real-time controller.
