## cover
Good morning. This is our progress review after the proposal. I will cover four things: the problem and our aim, the simulator and how we validated it against published results, first results on the residual Koopman approach we proposed, and first results on how long fault detection can take before recovery is no longer possible.

## recap
Our project has one aim with two parts. The first part is the approach from the proposal we presented: build the known physics of the spinning, failed vehicle into the model, and let a Koopman model learn only what the physics gets wrong, then use it inside MPC. The second part concerns timing. While reviewing the literature we found that recovery methods with guarantees assume the failure is detected instantly, but real detectors take tens to hundreds of milliseconds. We therefore also ask for a guaranteed bound on that delay, using Lyapunov and LMI tools from this course. The two parts connect: whichever controller catches the vehicle after detection, we can compare them by the delay they tolerate. Nothing from the proposal was dropped.

## timeline
When one rotor dies, a quadrotor cannot hover level any more. Mueller and D'Andrea showed it can still fly by spinning steadily about a tilted axis, called the relaxed hover. But there is a gap in time: the fault detector needs some time tau to notice the failure. During that time the normal controller keeps flying a vehicle that has lost a rotor. Only after detection does the failure controller take over and try to capture the vehicle into the spinning state. Most guarantee-based papers assume tau equals zero. Başak and Prempain proved stability for a linear model with a fixed delay, but without thrust limits and without finding the largest tolerable delay. Fast detectors take 30 to 130 milliseconds.

## model
We wrote our own simulator in Python. It is a full six degree of freedom rigid body with the rotor gyroscopic terms from the paper, actuator limits, and motor lag. The key state for the failed vehicle is the reduced attitude n: the vertical direction expressed in body axes. Because the vehicle spins, we control this direction rather than the full attitude. Every vehicle parameter comes from the Mueller and D'Andrea paper, so we can compare against their numbers directly. For the learning experiments we also build "truth" plants with effects the nominal model does not have.

## validation
Before trusting any result we checked the simulator against published numbers. The two-rotor and three-rotor-loss equilibria from Mueller and D'Andrea's ICRA paper match every digit they published. The three-rotor case is a strong check because it uses all the gyroscopic terms. For the one-rotor case, thrust, spin rate and tilt match within half a percent, but the pitch rate is six percent lower; we found that the paper's own published values leave a residual in its own equation 12, so that one published case is slightly inconsistent. We also wrote an independent implementation of their more detailed IJRR model and reproduced all five failure cases within 4 percent, and matched a second drone, the Bebop 2, from Sun et al. Finally, against flight data: with the thrust the flight actually used, read from their Figure 4, our model spins up to 10 radians per second in 0.65 seconds, against 0.62 in flight. The spin-up time depends strongly on that thrust, so the delay results later are shown for two thrust values.

## equilibrium
This is the equilibrium the failed vehicle must reach. With rotor 4 gone, the two side rotors produce equal thrust and the opposite rotor about half of it. The vehicle then spins at about 19.6 radians per second about an axis tilted 16 degrees from the body z axis, and the centre of mass moves on a circle of only about 7 millimetres. The figure shows how the equilibrium changes with the thrust ratio; it reproduces the paper's Figure 3.

## structure
Linearizing about the spinning equilibrium in body coordinates gives a linear time-invariant model. The open loop has two undamped oscillations, at 10 and 19.6 radians per second, the slow yaw mode, and the altitude double integrator. It is fully controllable from the three rotors, which reproduces the paper's result. On observability: gyros alone cannot see tilt or altitude. A small finding of ours: because the spinning equilibrium is tilted, vertical acceleration depends on tilt to first order, so gyro plus barometer is enough in principle. But with realistic sensor noise the observability Gramian shows it is about 2,700 times weaker than measuring tilt directly, so it is a structural property, not a practical estimator.

## lqr
We designed two LQR controllers: a normal hover controller, and a failure-mode controller at the spinning equilibrium using the paper's weights, with motor states and altitude added. On the full nonlinear model with motor lag and thrust limits, the failure controller recovers from a perturbed start: the altitude settles in 1.8 seconds, the tilt in 2.1 and the yaw rate in 2.5. One number to note: the failure controller tolerates only about 18 milliseconds of pure input delay, compared with 40 for the healthy one. The least-damped mode is the oscillation at the spin frequency, with a damping ratio of about 0.19.

## part1
Now the first part: learning only the mismatch of the physics model.

## koopman
We compare three predictors on the same data, exactly as proposed. The analytical model is the Mueller and D'Andrea linearization with no learning. Full-state EDMDc learns everything from data with a quadratic dictionary of 55 lifted states. The residual model keeps the physics and learns only the one-step error, clipped to a bound, as in Todde et al. 2026. To avoid learning our own code back, the data come from a "truth" plant with effects the physics model does not have. We test on held-out episodes from the same plant and from a different plant.

## prediction
Here is the main prediction result. On held-out episodes from the training plant, the residual model has the lowest error at every horizon from 100 milliseconds upward; at shorter horizons the full-state model is slightly better. At one second the residual error is 0.64, against 0.91 for full-state EDMDc and 7.1 for the analytical model, which is worse than simply assuming nothing changes, because the unmodelled thrust loss makes it drift. Our proposal claimed that full-state EDMDc is unstable; on the failed vehicle we confirm that it has eigenvalues outside the unit circle and that it falls behind the no-change baseline by two seconds. At two seconds even the residual model is behind no-change: long open-loop horizons need feedback, which is why the MPC re-plans every 5 milliseconds.

## limits
Two more results, one good and one bad. Good: the residual model needs less data. With only 10 short episodes it already beats the full-state model trained on 40. That matters because real failure data are scarce: failed flights tend to end in crashes. Bad: neither learned model generalizes to a plant with different parameters. On the held-out plant both are worse than the physics model alone. Training on randomized plants helps the residual model on the new plant only up to about 100 milliseconds, and makes both models worse on the training plant, because a static model cannot tell from a single state which vehicle it is on. This motivates our next step: adapt the residual online. Since the residual is linear in its parameters, recursive least squares is a natural, cheap choice.

## mpc
Now in closed loop. We built a linear MPC on the analytical model, and the same MPC with the residual added as a measured disturbance, evaluated outside the optimization so the QP stays convex. On the training plant, the mismatch causes a steady altitude error of about 29 centimetres for LQR and analytical MPC. The residual-Koopman MPC cuts it to 19 centimetres, a 32 percent reduction, and it helps for each separate mismatch effect of that plant. On the held-out plant it helps overall, but when we remove that plant's thrust loss it makes the error 47 percent larger, which is the generalization problem again. The solver needs under a millisecond per step; our modelling layer adds overhead, so a real-time version would call the solver directly. When we used the MPCs to catch the vehicle after a detection delay, they did not beat LQR: LQR was safe up to 10 milliseconds, the MPCs only without delay. The likely reason is that the linear model is not valid far from the spin. The obvious next baseline is integral action, which we will add.

## part2
The second part: how long can fault detection take?

## undetected
Why does the delay matter so much? When rotor 4 dies, rotor 2 on the opposite side is no longer balanced, which creates a roll torque. The healthy controller reacts by cutting rotor 2, but a running motor cannot go below its idle thrust. So a roll acceleration of at least 10.6 radians per second squared remains, whatever the tuning. The plot shows three tunings, all above this lower bound. After 100 milliseconds the vehicle is already tilted about 13 degrees, and after 200 about 37.

## delay
This is our key result so far on timing. We simulate the Mueller and D'Andrea strategy: spin up on two rotors, then switch to the LQR. For each delay we run 40 random pre-failure states and count the safe recoveries; the bands are 95 percent confidence intervals. With the thrust used in flight and instant detection, the vehicle climbs 40 centimetres and loses only 3 centimetres below its start, like their flight. With delay, things fall apart quickly: the probability of a safe recovery drops below 0.9 at about 12 milliseconds and below one half at about 19. With the lower, hover-neutral thrust these numbers are 7 and 14 milliseconds. Real detectors take 30 to 130 milliseconds. If we only ask for the attitude to be recovered, ignoring the altitude loss, the 50 percent point is at 52 milliseconds. These numbers hold for this strategy and model; nonlinear controllers in the literature have recovered with 100 milliseconds or more.

## certificate
To turn the delay question into a guarantee, we need a certified set of states from which the failure controller always recovers despite thrust limits. Our first version uses the Hu and Lin saturation conditions as LMIs on the linear model at the spinning equilibrium. It certifies about 22 percent of the way from the equilibrium to the state right after a failure in hover; simulation shows recovery up to 70 percent, so it is about three times conservative. The slice shows why: the controller only fails at spin rates of about 5 radians per second or less, but a certificate built at the full spin cannot reach there. That is exactly the gap our next step addresses, by treating the yaw rate as a varying parameter and certifying over the whole spin-up. These are certificates for the linear model; the nonlinear gap is measured by simulation.

## plan
Here is the plan for the remaining weeks, up to the final presentation on 31 October and the final report on 13 November. On the learning side we still owe the second controller from the proposal, MPC on the full-state Koopman model, plus the classical integral-action baseline, then online adaptation to fix the generalization problem. On the timing side the next step is the certificate over the whole spin-up, then the reachable set of the undetected phase, which together give the certified delay budget. The main risk is that the spin-up certificate is infeasible or too conservative; we have two fallbacks ready.

## summary
To summarize. Our simulator is validated against three papers and against the flight spin-up time. The residual Koopman model is the best predictor from 100 milliseconds on, needs less data, and improves MPC, but it does not yet generalize, and we have a concrete plan for that. On timing, the published recovery strategy is safe with high probability only for detection delays up to about 12 milliseconds in simulation, well below what real detectors need, which is the gap our certified delay budget will address. Thank you, we are happy to take questions.
