## cover
Good morning. This is our progress review. I will cover four things: the problem and our research question, the simulator and how we validated it against published results, our results on learning the error of the physics model, and the plan for the remaining weeks.

## timeline
First, the problem. A quadrotor has no spare actuator: its four thrusts control exactly four things, height, roll, pitch and yaw. The propellers also twist the body with small reaction torques, and in normal flight these cancel because two propellers spin each way. When one rotor fails, they no longer cancel, so the drone cannot avoid spinning. Mueller and D'Andrea showed that this is not the end. If we give up the heading, the remaining three rotors can hold the drone in a steady spin of about 19 radians per second about an axis tilted 16 degrees, like a spinning top. They called it the relaxed hover, proved it is controllable and flew it on a real drone.

## problem
So why is there still a problem? A controller is only as good as its model. The Mueller and D'Andrea model captures the main spinning motion, but it misses real effects: in their flights the drone produced less thrust than expected while spinning fast and spun 20 to 27 percent slower than predicted, inertias are never known exactly, and motor speed varies. A controller built on the model then settles with a steady error. Our research question is therefore: can a model that learns only this mismatch, on top of the physics model, predict the drone more accurately and give a smaller steady error in MPC than the physics model alone, or than a Koopman model that learns everything? Koopman methods describe the state by many nonlinear features, here all pairwise products, and fit a linear model on them by least squares. We use the same tools to learn the one-step error of the physics model. The learned correction is evaluated outside the MPC optimization, so the MPC stays a convex quadratic program. Residual Koopman models exist for healthy drones and other robots, but we did not find them for the spinning regime after complete rotor loss.

## model
We wrote our own simulator in Python. It is a full six degree of freedom rigid body with the rotor gyroscopic terms from the paper, actuator limits, and motor lag. The key state for the failed vehicle is the reduced attitude n: the vertical direction expressed in body axes. Because the vehicle spins, we control this direction rather than the full attitude. Every vehicle parameter comes from the Mueller and D'Andrea paper, so we can compare against their numbers directly. For the learning experiments we also build "truth" plants with effects the nominal model does not have.

## validation
Before trusting any result we checked the simulator against published numbers. The two-rotor and three-rotor-loss equilibria from Mueller and D'Andrea's ICRA paper match every digit they published. The three-rotor case is a strong check because it uses all the gyroscopic terms. For the one-rotor case, thrusts, yaw rate and the vertical component of the axis match within half a percent, but the pitch rate and tilt are about six and five percent lower; we found that the paper's own published values leave a residual in its own equation 12, so that one published case is slightly inconsistent. We also wrote an independent implementation of their more detailed IJRR model and reproduced all five published hover cases within 4 percent, and matched a second drone, the Bebop 2, from Sun et al. Finally, against flight data: with the thrust the flight actually used, read from their Figure 4, our model spins up to 10 radians per second in 0.65 seconds, against 0.62 in flight. The spin-up time depends strongly on that thrust, which is why we read it from the flight data rather than assuming half the weight per rotor.

## equilibrium
This is the equilibrium the failed vehicle must reach. With rotor 4 gone, the two side rotors produce equal thrust and the opposite rotor about half of it. The vehicle then spins at about 19.6 radians per second about an axis tilted 16 degrees from the body z axis, and the centre of mass moves on a circle of only about 7 millimetres. The figure shows how the equilibrium changes with the thrust ratio; it reproduces the paper's Figure 3.

## structure
Linearizing about the spinning equilibrium in body coordinates gives a linear time-invariant model. The open loop has two undamped oscillations, at 10 and 19.6 radians per second, the slow yaw mode, and the altitude double integrator. It is fully controllable from the three rotors, which reproduces the paper's result. On observability: gyros alone cannot see tilt or altitude. A small finding of ours: because the spinning equilibrium is tilted, vertical acceleration depends on tilt to first order, so gyro plus barometer is enough in principle. But with realistic sensor noise the observability Gramian shows it is about 2,700 times weaker than measuring tilt directly, so it is a structural property, not a practical estimator.

## lqr
We designed two LQR controllers: a normal hover controller, and a failure-mode controller at the spinning equilibrium using the paper's weights, with motor states and altitude added. On the full nonlinear model with motor lag and thrust limits, the failure controller recovers from a perturbed start: the altitude settles in 1.8 seconds, the tilt in 2.1 and the yaw rate in 2.5. One number to note: the failure controller tolerates only about 18 milliseconds of pure input delay, compared with 40 for the healthy one. The least-damped mode is the oscillation at the spin frequency, with a damping ratio of about 0.19.

## part1
Now our approach: learn only the mismatch of the physics model, and use it inside MPC.

## koopman
We compare three predictors on the same data, exactly as proposed. The analytical model is the Mueller and D'Andrea linearization with no learning. Full-state EDMDc learns everything from data with a quadratic dictionary of 55 lifted states. The residual model keeps the physics and learns only the one-step error, clipped to a bound, as in Todde et al. 2026. To avoid learning our own code back, the data come from a "truth" plant with effects the physics model does not have. We test on held-out episodes from the same plant and from a different plant.

## prediction
Here is the main prediction result. On held-out episodes from the training plant, the residual model has the lowest error of the three models from 100 milliseconds on, and is the best predictor overall from 100 milliseconds to one second; at shorter horizons the full-state model is better, about two and a half times at 20 milliseconds. At one second the residual error is 0.64, against 0.91 for full-state EDMDc and 7.1 for the analytical model, which is worse than simply assuming nothing changes, because the unmodelled thrust loss makes it drift. Our proposal claimed that full-state EDMDc is unstable; on the failed vehicle we confirm that it has eigenvalues outside the unit circle and that it falls behind the no-change baseline by two seconds. At two seconds even the residual model is behind no-change: long open-loop horizons need feedback, which is why the MPC re-plans every 5 milliseconds.

## limits
Two more results, one good and one bad. Good: the residual model needs less data. With only 10 short episodes it already beats the full-state model trained on 40. That matters because real failure data are scarce: failed flights tend to end in crashes. Bad: neither learned model generalizes to a plant with different parameters. On the held-out plant both are worse than the physics model alone. Training on randomized plants helps the residual model on the new plant only up to about 100 milliseconds, and makes both models worse on the training plant, because a static model cannot tell from a single state which vehicle it is on. This motivates our next step: adapt the residual online. Since the residual is linear in its parameters, recursive least squares is a natural, cheap choice.

## mpc
Now in closed loop. We built a linear MPC on the analytical model, and the same MPC with the learned residual added as a measured disturbance, evaluated outside the optimization so the QP stays convex. On the training plant, the mismatch causes a steady altitude error of about 29 centimetres for LQR and analytical MPC. The residual Koopman MPC cuts it to 19 centimetres, a 32 percent reduction, and it helps for each separate mismatch effect of that plant. On the held-out plant it helps overall, but when we remove that plant's thrust loss it makes the error 47 percent larger, which is the generalization problem again. The solver needs under a millisecond per step; our modelling layer adds overhead, so a real-time version would call the solver directly. Honestly, the most important missing baseline is integral action, or offset-free MPC, which in theory removes a constant bias completely; we will compare against it next.

## plan
Here is the plan up to the final presentation on 31 October and the final report on 13 November. We still owe the second controller from the proposal, MPC on the full-state Koopman model, and the classical integral-action baseline. Then we address the main weakness, generalization, with online adaptation: the residual is linear in its parameters, so recursive least squares is a natural, cheap choice. We will also make the truth plant more realistic with air-relative thrust and train the residual on spin-up transients. The main risk is that online adaptation needs richer data than a single flight gives; the fallback is a residual that also uses a short history of states.

## summary
To summarize. Our simulator is validated against three papers and against the flight spin-up time. Learning only the error of the physics model gives the best predictions from 100 milliseconds to one second, and with 10 training episodes it beats a full Koopman model trained on 40. Inside MPC it cuts the altitude bias by a third, but it does not yet generalize to a different vehicle, and online adaptation is our next step. Thank you, we are happy to take questions.
